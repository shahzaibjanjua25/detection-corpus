# Hypothesis: T1070.002 — Log Clearing / Tampering

## Statement
If an adversary completes their objective on a Linux host, their first
anti-forensic move is to destroy the evidence: truncate auth.log, vacuum
the journal, or rm /var/log/* entirely. This is the last thing they do
before exiting, and it is the single most damaging act from a defender's
perspective — a successful log clear can erase the entire intrusion trail.

## Observable
Two independent signals OR'd together:
  (a) An auditd PATH record naming /var/log/** paired with a destructive
      SYSCALL (unlink, rename, truncate, ftruncate).
  (b) An EXECVE of journalctl --vacuum-*, rm /var/log, truncate/shred/dd
      against /var/log, or journalctl --rotate/--flush.
Excludes logrotate invocations, package managers, config-management
tooling, and systemd-journald itself.

## Why process_creation + auditd, not just one
Log clearing happens two ways: (1) the adversary directly manipulates
files (auditd PATH+SYSCALL catches this), and (2) the adversary invokes
the system's own log tooling (auditd may not emit a PATH record for the
*inside* of journalctl's vacuum — it calls unlink internally on files the
rule doesn't directly watch, but the EXECVE of journalctl with --vacuum-*
is unambiguous). A single-source rule misses half the attack surface. This
is the first rule in the series to require two log sources, and it's the
reason the rule is authored under `service: auditd` but documents the
process_creation branch as equally load-bearing.

## Telemetry prerequisite
auditd watch on log paths, e.g.:
  -w /var/log/ -p wa -k log_clear
  -w /var/log/audit/ -p wa -k log_clear
  -w /var/log/journal/ -p wa -k log_clear
  -w /run/log/journal/ -p wa -k log_clear
Note: `-p wa` covers write and attribute changes; `unlink` and `truncate`
are NOT captured by `-p wa` on all auditd/kernel combinations. Verify with
a test truncate and a test rm on a watched path; if PATH records are
missing, add `-p wad` or use a separate watch. This is the second rule in
the series where the audit watch scope has a measurable cost — /var/log is
busy on any host with active services.

## Why it beats alerting after the fact
Every subsequent investigation depends on the logs. Firing at the moment
of destruction — before the adversary has finished clearing — gives
responders a chance to snapshot /var/log, freeze the host, or pull remote
log copies before they are lost. This is the one rule in the series where
the *timing* of the alert matters more than the fidelity.

## Known blind spots
- Adversary who clears logs by rewriting them in place (e.g., editing
  auth.log to remove specific lines with `sed -i`) may not trigger a
  destructive SYSCALL — sed writes to a temp file and renames, which IS
  covered by rename*, but in-place editors (e.g., some vi configurations)
  may not be. This is a genuine gap; a content-integrity check on log
  files (e.g., AIDE or a hash baseline) is the only reliable complement.
- Adversary who disables logging first (systemctl stop auditd, kill -9
  rsyslogd) → the rule fires on the *stop*, not the clear. That is a
  separate rule (T1562.001, planned Day 9) and should be correlated.
- Remote log shipping to a SIEM → the attacker can clear local logs but
  the SIEM copy survives, which is the whole point of remote shipping.
  This rule does not detect the *attempt* if the clear targets only local
  files; it detects the local destruction, which is still worth alerting on.
- Hosts without auditd → process_creation branch still works but the
  direct-file-manipulation branch is blind. Pair with a Falco rule on
  `open_write` / `unlink` for /var/log/**.
